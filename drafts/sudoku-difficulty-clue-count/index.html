Fewer Clues Doesn't Mean a Harder Sudoku: What 4,000 Puzzles Show

Almost every Sudoku app sets difficulty the same way: Easy shows lots of numbers, Hard shows fewer. It feels obvious — fewer clues, less to go on, harder puzzle. To check, we generated 4,000 Sudoku puzzles, solved every one of them the way a person would, and recorded the hardest technique each one actually required. The number of clues turned out to say almost nothing about how hard a puzzle is.

How we measured difficulty

Each puzzle was made the classic way. We built a complete, valid grid, then erased clues in random order, putting each one back if erasing it allowed a second solution. When no clue can be removed without breaking uniqueness, the puzzle is minimal — and that's what most generators hand to players.

Then a solver worked through each puzzle like a methodical human. At every step it tries the easiest technique first and only reaches for a harder one when nothing simpler makes progress:

  1. Hidden singles — the only cell in a row, column or box where a digit fits (scanning and cross-hatching).
  2. Naked singles — the only digit left for a cell.
  3. Naked and hidden pairs.
  4. Pointing pairs and box/line reduction.
  5. Naked and hidden triples.
  6. X-Wing — a digit confined to the same two columns in two rows (or the reverse), which rules it out of the rest of those columns.

The hardest technique the solver was forced to use is the puzzle's difficulty. If the six techniques together still couldn't finish it, we marked it "beyond": it needs something more advanced, like a Swordfish or a chain.

Clue count barely predicts difficulty — and points the wrong way

If fewer clues meant harder puzzles, the 22-clue bar would be the darkest. It's the other way round. Among 22-clue puzzles, 44% needed only singles and 31% defeated the whole toolbox; among 27-clue puzzles, only 31% fell to singles and 56% defeated the toolbox. The correlation between clue count and difficulty is 0.11, on a scale where 0 means no relationship and 1 a perfect one — weak, and pointing the opposite way to the folklore.

There's a subtlety here. Take any one puzzle and erase another clue, and it can only get harder or stay the same. But different puzzles are different machines. One reading: a minimal puzzle that needs 27 clues to pin down a single solution is one whose clues give each other little help, so each clue does less work for the solver. Puzzles that needed only singles averaged 24.3 clues; puzzles beyond the toolbox averaged 24.6. Counting won't tell them apart.

The board on the left has fewer clues and is the easier of the two by a mile. Difficulty lives in where the clues sit and how they interact, not in how many there are. A clue in the right place can unlock a whole box; seven clues in the wrong places can leave every row, column and box ambiguous.

Hard techniques are rare; "beyond" is common

Two numbers stand out. First, only 0.6% of puzzles needed a triple or an X-Wing — about one in 174. Those are the puzzles that make the hardest standard techniques worth learning, and a generator that simply removes clues almost never produces one by chance.

Second, 39% — two puzzles in five — needed something beyond all six techniques. That's the hidden cost of rating by clue count. Give a player a random minimal puzzle labelled "Hard" and there's a real chance it needs techniques most players have never heard of. From the solver's chair that feels exactly like a puzzle that requires guessing, and it's how a lot of people come to believe hard Sudoku is luck.

How we set difficulty instead

So on Sudoku Zen, the technique sets the level, not the clue count. The generator removes clues one at a time and keeps a removal only if the puzzle can still be finished using the techniques allowed for that level. A Hard board can be finished with pairs, so you never need more than pairs. Then it checks that the board really needs pairs, and starts again if singles would have been enough. Because the whole board is solvable by logic, it also has exactly one solution.

Triples and X-Wings are rare enough that searching for them live would leave you waiting a few seconds, so Master and Extreme boards come from a bank the same generator built offline. Each one is relabelled and reshuffled before you see it — the digits swapped and the rows and columns rearranged within their bands — which gives billions of distinct-looking boards with the same logical difficulty.

A correction. Running this analysis turned up a problem in our own game. Until November 2026, Sudoku Zen set difficulty by clue count alone and didn't check every board for a single solution. Tested on the old code, 95% of Hard boards and nearly all Expert, Master and Extreme boards had more than one valid solution — so a correct digit could be marked as a mistake. Every board is now generated the way this article describes.

What this means when you play

  • Judge a puzzle by its first stall, not its first glance. A sparse grid can fall to scanning; a busy one can stop you dead. The clue count tells you how much is filled in, not how hard it is.
  • Learn techniques in order. Singles finish more than two in five random puzzles. Pairs and pointing pairs carry you a long way after that. Triples and X-Wings are rare but decisive when they turn up.
  • If you're stuck on one of our boards, there's a technique you haven't spotted. Every board is rated by the hardest technique it needs, so the move is always there.

The generator, the rating solver and the analysis scripts are in our public repository — the game runs the same code.
